# Abstract 

With the emergence of Physical AI, artificial intelligence is extending beyond screen-based applications to embodied systems that perceive, interact with, and act in the physical world. Unlike traditional AI, Physical AI operates under real-time safety constraints, continuously interacts with dynamic environments, and coexists with humans. While current governance approaches have largely been developed for digital AI systems, they do not explicitly account for embodiment, physical safety, lifecycle adaptation, or human–machine interaction. To bridge this gap, we present a comprehensive survey of Physical AI governance that integrates both scientific and operational perspectives. We first develop a governance framework that unifies foundational governance principles. We then derive a five-stage Physical AI lifecycle and demonstrate how governance can be systematically operationalized across research, design, data, model development, and deployment. By translating governance principles into actionable implementation practices, our paper provides a systematic foundation for developing Physical AI systems that are safe, trustworthy, and socially beneficial while supporting continued scientific and technological advancement. 

Keywords: Physical AI life cycle, physical AI governance

3.1 Robust & Safe Operation 

Robust and Safe Operation ensures that Physical AI systems perform their intended functions safely, reliably, and adaptively throughout their operational lifetime. Safety and security prevent accidental harm and protect against malicious threats Li et al. (2026b). Reliability emphasizes dependable and consistent system performance Hou et al. (2026). Adaptability enables systems to respond effectively to dynamic environments, unforeseen conditions, and evolving tasks Xiang et al. (2026). These components provide the technical 5foundation for trustworthy Physical AI as an infrastructure layer by ensuring resilient operation while minimizing risks to humans, infrastructure, and the environment. 

Robustness, Efficiency, Safety & Security covers a system’s ability to react in real time, avoid causing physical harm, and to resist malicious interference or manipulation, given the uncertainties and potential defection in the environment Song et al. (2023). In traditional AI governance, robustness and efficiency are primarily viewed as performance or success metrics that ensure reliable computational performance and protect digital models. However, in Physical AI governance, they become foundational capabilities that enable safe and reliable operation in the physical world by preventing, detecting, and mitigating harmful consequences in a timely manner.International Organization for Standardization (2022). For safety and security, two aspects are important: physical-safety and cyber security dimensions. Physical safety ensures that AI systems do not cause harm to humans or the environment during operation, and cyber security protects systems against malicious attacks that could compromise their behavior or integrity International Organization for Standardization (2025; 2016; 2022); International Organization for Standardization and International Electrotechnical Commission (2022); National Institute of Standards and Technology (2023). The goal of these governance factors are to maintain resilient performance, satisfy real-time constraints, prevent physical harm, and protect against cyber-physical threats Lee et al. (2025).s 

Stability, Consistency & Reliability refers to the ability of a Physical AI system to consistently perform its intended functions under expected operating conditions while maintaining predictable behavior and avoiding unexpected failures Song et al. (2023). It is important to establish standardized benchmarks and evaluation metrics, supported by iterative verification, simulation, testing, and runtime monitoring throughout the life cycle. Benchmark datasets, validated tool chains, and large-scale industrial validation further enable systematic assessment of system reliability, consistency, and performance under both expected and edge-case operating conditions Araujo et al. (2023). In addition, governance should promote resilient system architectures that support anomaly detection, graceful degradation, fault recovery, adaptive reconfiguration, and continuous performance assurance, enabling Physical AI systems to maintain dependable operation despite failures, attacks, and environmental uncertainty Segovia-Ferreira et al. (2024). 

Adaptability is the capability of a system to adjust its behavior in response to changing environments, tasks, or data distributions without requiring full retraining. The goal of adaptability is to ensure that Physical AI systems continuously perceive, reason, plan, and act in response to dynamic and uncertain environments, enabling them to autonomously adjust their behavior while preserving safety, reliability, robustness, and task performance across diverse real-world scenarios Tang et al. (2025). Adaptability should be continuously evaluated through diverse environmental benchmarks, scenario-based testing, and runtime performance monitoring to ensure that adaptive behaviors remain aligned with system objectives Zhang et al. (2025b). 

3.2 Human-Centered Values 

Physical AI is transforming autonomous systems from task-executing machines into context-aware collaborators that adapt to dynamic environments, augment human capabilities, and enable more intelligent interactions across diverse real-world domains. This evolution elevates human-centeredness from a design consideration to a core governance principle Singh et al. (2026). Advances in multimodal perception, adaptive control, and machine learning are shifting robotics from rigid automation toward human-aware, context-sensitive collaboration in unstructured environments Zhao et al. (2025). Advances in Physical AI are enabling robots to perceive, interpret, and respond to human emotions, facilitating more natural socially intelligent interactions Duncan et al. (2024). 

Human-Centered means putting people first. It is a design and governance philosophy that ensure technology serves humans, rather than expecting humans to adapt to technology Shneiderman (2020). First, human should be in the loop. Putting humans at the center of design thinking rather than emulating autonomous human-like agents, systems should be designed to keep people in control while automating discrete sub-tasks, with human self-efficacy and oversight as explicit goals. Second, physical AI should be driven by human purpose. Human-centered is an approach to interactive systems development that aims to make systems usable and useful by focusing on the users, their needs and requirements, and by applying human factors and ergonomics, usability knowledge and techniques. This approach improves efficiency and 6effectiveness, improves human well-being, user satisfaction, accessibility, and sustainability, and counteracts the possible adverse effects of use on human health, safety, and performance International Organization for Standardization (2019). 

Value Alignment ensures Physical AI aligns with human intentions, values, and preferences while adapting to human oversight and evolving contexts Gabriel (2020). Understanding and value alignment in Physical AI require embodied interaction with the real world rather than solely learning from textual data. Embodied AI agents develop an understanding of not only factual knowledge but also the values that guide appropriate actions through continuous perception, interaction, adaptation, and experience. This alignment is an evolutionary, procedural, and context-dependent process, enabling agents to learn human-compatible values, adapt to changing environments, and evolve beyond predefined rules. Compared with rule-based alignment methods (e.g., RLHF) used for large language models, embodiment provides richer feedback that supports more natural, efficient, and intuitive value learning while reducing biases and improving the effectiveness of human oversight Paolo et al. (2024). 

Accessibility is the requirement that AI systems be usable by people with a wide range of abilities, including those with disabilities Qbilat et al. (2021); Nanavati et al. (2024). Accessibility ensures that Physical AI systems are designed with inclusive interfaces, adaptive autonomy, and user-centered interactions that accommodate diverse abilities, enabling people with disabilities to safely, effectively, and independently interact with embodied AI Nanavati et al. (2024). Accessibility should be embedded from the earliest stages of Physical AI development through co-design with people with disabilities. By actively involving end users developers can create product that better address diverse user needs, improve usability, and promote equitable access Han et al. (2024). 

3.3 Integrity, Privacy and Equity 

Integrity, Privacy, and Equity are fundamental principles for the responsible governance of data in Physical AI. Integrity ensures that the multimodal data used by Physical AI systems are authentic, accurate, complete, and consistently maintained throughout sensing, transmission, storage, and learning. Maintaining data integrity is critical because corrupted or low-quality sensor data can directly compromise perception, planning, and safe interaction with the physical world Mehak et al. (2024). Privacy requires Physical AI systems to protect sensitive multimodal information collected through cameras, microphones, wearable sensors, and other embodied sensing devices by preserving confidentiality, user anonymity, and appropriate control over data collection and use. Because Physical AI operates in real-world environments, privacy extends beyond data protection to include physical, psychological, and social dimensions of human interactionCallander et al. (2024). Equity ensures that the data used to train and evaluate Physical AI systems are representative of diverse users, environments, and interaction contexts, thereby reducing systematic biases and promoting fair and reliable performance across different populations and real-world deployment conditions. For embodied agents, achieving equity also requires collecting data beyond controlled laboratory settings to better capture the diversity of real-world environments Niu et al. (2024). 

Responsible Data ensures that data used throughout the Physical AI lifecycle are collected, processed, and managed in an ethical, lawful, and representative manner while minimizing bias and discrimination Rivadeneira et al. (2024). This requires robust data governance practices, including data quality assurance, privacy protection, informed consent, secure data management, and compliance with applicable regulations Fan et al. (2026). Physical AI systems should also be trained and evaluated on diverse and representative datasets to mitigate algorithmic bias, promote equitable performance across different users and environments, and ensure fair, reliable, and trustworthy decision-making in real-world deployments Perlo et al. (2025). 

Privacy and Confidentiality is the protection of individuals’ personal information from unauthorized collection, use, or disclosure within a system’s data pipeline. For Physical AI, the diverse data used for training introduces significant privacy challenges, including data authentication, data breaches, and risks to user anonymity. For infrastructure, it is important to apply a framework for understanding how information flows across these systems, from the underlying physical and network infrastructure to the application layer that enables immersive virtual experiences Pereira et al. (2025). For physical device, privacy and confidentiality should be also addressed through secure device authentication, data encryption, secure communication, 7and the constraints of resource-limited edge devices while safeguarding sensitive information collected from continuous sensing and human interaction Bandi (2023). Techniques such as privacy-preserving architecture can be applied to minimize performance degradation Rivadeneira et al. (2024). 

Bias and Representativeness ensure that the data used to train and evaluate Physical AI systems adequately capture the diversity of users, environments, and interaction contexts while minimizing systematic biases. Because Physical AI relies on multimodal sensor data and operates in dynamic real-world environments, unrepresentative datasets can degrade perception, manipulation, navigation, and human–robot interaction, leading to unfair performance across different demographic groups and deployment scenarios. Addressing dataset bias through diverse data collection, balanced evaluation, and inclusive benchmark design is therefore essential for achieving equitable, robust, and reliable Physical AI systems Vöneky et al. (2024). 

Fairness requires Physical AI systems to provide equitable and non-discriminatory performance across diverse users, environments, and interaction contexts, ensuring that system behavior does not systematically disadvantage particular individuals or groups Perlo et al. (2025). As an example, robots inherits gender and racial biases from algorithm or large language models with common stereotypes, affecting real-world task execution. An experiment asked robot to pack the criminal block in the brown box. There is no image of a criminal. Instead, there are two blocks showing photos of a self-identified Black man and a self-identified White man. The robot incorrectly associates criminality with appearance. This results demonstrates how biases learned by AI models can cause embodied AI systems to perform discriminatory physical actions, incorrectly associating criminality with race and resulting in unfair treatment of different individuals Hundt et al. (2022). 

3.4 Accountability & Oversight 

Accountability and oversight ensures that humans remain responsible for the behavior and consequences of Physical AI systems by enabling effective supervision, intervention, and governance throughout the system lifecycle. Unlike traditional AI, Physical AI operates autonomously in the physical world and directly interacts with people and infrastructure, making continuous human oversight essential for preventing unsafe behaviors, assigning responsibility, and maintaining public trust Takeda et al. (2020); Mezgár & Váncza (2022). 

Transparency & Accountability requires that the perception, reasoning, planning, and physical actions of Physical AI systems are understandable and attributable to developers, operators, regulators, and affected users. Unlike traditional AI, transparency in Physical AI extends beyond algorithmic decisions to include sensor observations, environmental representations, motion planning, control policies, hardware capabilities, and operational constraints that influence real-world behavior. Comprehensive documentation of system design, training data provenance, embodiment assumptions, safety constraints, and deployment conditions enables stakeholders to understand why a system performed a particular action, assign responsibility for its outcomes, and support effective human oversight throughout the Physical AI lifecycle Billard & Kragić (2019); International Organization for Standardization (2025); Callander et al. (2024). 

Control requires Physical AI systems to incorporate mechanisms that enable humans to monitor, guide, intervene in, override, or safely deactivate system operations whenever necessary. Because Physical AI continuously interacts with dynamic environments, control mechanisms must support both routine supervision and emergency intervention while maintaining stable and reliable system behavior. Human involvement may range from direct teleoperation and shared autonomy to supervisory control over highly autonomous systems, depending on the deployment context and associated risks Billard & Kragić (2019). Adaptive control is essential for ensuring stable and reliable operation in both deterministic and continuous-time systems, as well as stochastic discrete-time systems. There are five levels of control in Physical AI, representing a continuum from fully human-controlled systems to fully autonomous operation. These levels range from human control, AI-assisted control, AI-advisory control, and AI-collaborative control, to AI-active independent control, where humans transition from direct decision-makers to supervisory roles Mezgár & Váncza (2022). 

Copyright & Compliance ensures that Physical AI systems satisfy applicable legal, regulatory, safety, and intellectual property requirements throughout their lifecycle. Beyond complying with AI governance frameworks, Physical AI must also conform to robotics safety standards, machinery regulations, functional safety requirements, and copyright obligations associated with training data, software, digital assets, and 8foundation models. Because Physical AI directly interacts with humans and the physical environment, compliance additionally requires verifying that sensing, planning, control, and actuation behaviors satisfy safety certification, operational constraints, and continuous risk management before and during deployment National Institute of Standards and Technology (2023). 

Explainability is the capability of Physical AI systems to provide human-understandable explanations for their perceptions, reasoning processes, planning decisions, and executed physical actions. Unlike traditional AI, where explanations often focus on prediction outcomes, Physical AI must explain how multimodal sensor observations, environmental context, motion planning, task objectives, and safety constraints jointly contributed to a specific physical behavior. Such explanations improve operator trust, facilitate debugging and failure diagnosis, support safe human–robot collaboration, and provide evidence for regulatory review and accident investigation Billard & Kragić (2019). 

Auditability is the capability to trace, reconstruct, verify, and evaluate the behavior of Physical AI systems through comprehensive records of data provenance, model development, system configuration, sensor observations, world-state estimates, planning decisions, control commands, and execution logs. Because Physical AI continuously interacts with dynamic environments, audit trails must capture both computational decisions and physical system behaviors to enable failure analysis, responsibility attribution, compliance verification, and continuous safety improvement. Comprehensive logging and standardized documentation therefore support post-incident investigation, regulatory compliance, and the trustworthy deployment of embodied AI systems throughout their operational lifecycle International Organization for Standardization (2023). 

3.5 Sustainability 

Sustainability is the principle that Physical AI systems should be designed, developed, deployed, and retired in ways that minimize environmental impacts while maximizing the efficient use of resources throughout their lifecycle. Because Physical AI combines computational intelligence with physical hardware, sustainable governance extends beyond energy-efficient AI models to include responsible material use, hardware longevity, maintenance, repairability, reuse, and end-of-life management. This ensures that embodied AI systems deliver long-term environmental, economic, and societal value while reducing resource consumption and waste Fink et al. (2023); Haddadin et al. (2024). 

Resource Efficiency requires Physical AI systems to minimize the consumption of computational, energy, and material resources throughout their lifecycle while maintaining reliable performance. Since embodied AI relies on energy-intensive training, onboard computation, sensors, actuators, and physical hardware, improving resource efficiency through energy-aware algorithms, efficient hardware design, adaptive computation, and optimized task execution is essential for reducing operational costs and environmental impact Fink et al. (2023). 

Circularity promotes designing Physical AI systems for longevity, modularity, repairability, reuse, remanufac-turing, and recycling to reduce material waste and extend hardware lifespans. Unlike traditional AI, Physical AI depends on physical components that require responsible lifecycle management. Adopting circular design principles enables efficient resource utilization, minimizes electronic waste, and supports environmentally sustainable deployment of robotic and embodied AI systems Haddadin et al. (2024). 

# 4 Operationalizing Governance Across the Physical AI Lifecycle 

In this section, we distill the lifecycle of Physical AI and survey existing research approaches to operationalizing governance throughout each stage of the life cycle. The lifecycle of Physical AI is inherently intertwined with its capabilitiesSun et al. (2024). Current key Physical AI capabilities include perceiving, cognition, planning, acting and controlling, predicting, self-learning, and remembering, navigation and manipulation (Table:2). These capabilities can be organized into distinct maturity levels based on four dimensions: degree of autonomy, task-handling complexity, environmental adaptability, and social/cognitive interaction ability Long et al. (2025). Capabilities require different degrees 9of hardware versus software support. Perceiving, for instance, is the ability to acquire information about the physical environment, realized through hardware mechanisms such as LiDAR, tactile sensors, multimodal sensor arrays, and state-estimation systems. The capabilities such as cognition and planning are comparatively hardware-light and instead depend heavily on software. Acting and controlling sits in between, since it depends on software-generated commands but is only realized through physical actuator hardware Neupane et al. (2024). Traditionally, the robot capabilities are delivered through a hierarchical or sequential architecture following sense–plan–act cycle. Physical system architectures incorporate large language models (LLMs) and vision-language-action (VLA) models as the robot’s cognitive core, significantly enhancing its ability to understand, reason, and navigate in unstructured environments Gaba et al. (2026). Given the early stage of Physical AI and the broad range of capabilities required for embodied intelligence, we distill the lifecycle into two overarching layers: the knowledge layer and the build and operation layer .The knowledge layer encompasses research and design that generate, validate, and disseminate foundational knowledge, algorithms, and best practices for Physical AI. The build and operation layer comprises data collection, model development, deployment, and continual operation, where these capabilities are implemented and refined in real-world environments. Governance considerations span the entire lifecycle while others are specific to particular stages. Moreover, the lifecycle is inherently iterative, with continuous feedback among research, development, deployment, and operational experience driving ongoing improvements in both system capabilities and governance practices. 

4.1 Key Physical AI Life Cycle Components 

As shown in Figure: 3, The Physical AI life cycle covers the same core stages as traditional AI but places significantly greater emphasis on iterative development, continuous feedback, and cross-stage interactions among research, design, data, models and deployment. 

Research The traditional scientific research process typically consists of several key stages: reviewing existing literature to understand prior findings, formulating research questions or hypotheses, designing and conducting studies or experiments, analyzing and validating the results, and finally deriving insights and making novel scientific contributions.Cooper (2017). In AI academia, research follows a lifecycle that includes ideation, planning, project execution and management, publication and knowledge dissemination, preserving, the formation of collaborative research communities and ecosystems, and re-use University Health Network, Michener Institute of Education (n.d.); Borgman (2019). In addition, there is no clear boundary between research and production. Rather, the transition is an iterative process. Some prior studies have proposed structured frameworks that bridge scientific research and production systems through staged maturity levels. These frameworks identify key transition points, including software readiness, research-to-product transfer, and system deployment readiness Lavin et al. (2022). Physical AI research follows the scientific method but extends it with embodiment, interaction with the environment Kober et al. (2013), and iterative experimentation. These processes form a continuous feedback loop that enables systems to adapt to the complexities of real-world environments. Furthermore, robotics research increasingly treats software, datasets, simulation environments, and hardware designs as first-class research outputs that accompany publications. Researchers increasingly release open-source code, pretrained models, simulation environments, robot operating software, benchmark datasets, hardware designs, and demonstration videos Bonsignorio et al. (2025). These complementary research artifacts improve reproducibility, accelerate community adoption, and promote collaborative innovation. 

Design Design in Physical AI is not a stand-alone activity but an iterative process that spans the entire system life cycle, with design decisions continuously refined through feedback from development, deployment, and operation. Physical AI design requires the co-design of AI models, software, and hardware rather than optimizing each component independently. Human-centered design remains a fundamental principle throughout the design process. Rather than replacing humans during design, Physical AI systems should keep humans in the loop by incorporating stakeholder input, expert evaluation, and iterative human feedback to guide system development and improve safety, usability, and performance (Nilles et al., 2018). First, different data modalities are employed at different stages of the life cycle with strengths and trade-offs. For instance, web-scale video data provide rich visual information and semantic world knowledge, but they lack explicit action annotations and physical interaction data. Consequently, models trained predominantly on passive video observations may exhibit poor spatial reasoning and fail to reliably model object interactions McCarthy et al. (2025). Second, different types of data can be combined to enhance both training efficiency and model performance. For instance, with a data pyramid, action-labeled and action-free data can be jointly used for co-training to learn a unified policy Bjorck et al. (2025); Lin et al. (2026). Third, continuous acquisition of high-quality data across diverse tasks, environments, and robot embodiments is important to improve model performance for generalization and scalability Chen et al. Model The defining advancement of Physical AI over traditional robotics is the emergence of an intelligent robot brain that enables robots to perceive, reason, and act in complex environments (Table:4). Physical AI models can be broadly organized into five functional aspects: perception, reasoning, control and skill learning, action generation, and world modeling. These functions are implemented by eight major model families. Vision-Language Foundation Models (VLMs) and visual representation models provide multimodal perception and semantic understanding. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, Robot Foundation Models (RFMs), and Large Behavior Models (LBMs) enable general robotic control and transferable skill learning. Diffusion and flow-based policy models generate robust action trajectories, while World Foundation Models (WFMs) support planning, simulation, and synthetic data generation. Finally, reasoning and planning models enable long-horizon decision-making, collectively forming the core intelligence stack of modern Physical AI systems Gaba et al. (2026); Firoozi et al. (2025). The model stage covers the activities that transform raw and curated data into a functioning learned system, spanning initial architecture through final validation. Architecture design and development (Firoozi et al., 2025) and model de velopment (Gaba et al., 2026) establish the underlying network structure and learning approach, after which Pre-training (Kawaharazuka et al., 2025) builds general-purpose representations, often at scale and across broad, task-agnostic data, before Fine-tuning (Kim et al., 2024) specializes the model to a target task or embodiment. Post-training (Kawaharazuka et al., 2025) further refines model behavior after the initial training pass, commonly through preference alignment or task-specific adaptation, while Prototyping (Bousmalis et al., 2023) and Sim-to-Real Transfer (Zhao et al., 2020) bridge the gap between simulated development environments and physical deployment, addressing the discrepancies that arise when a model trained in simulation must operate on real hardware. Optimization (Aljalbout et al., 2026) then improves model efficiency and performance for deployment constraints, and Testing and Validation (Yi et al., 2026) closes the stage by verifying the model meets performance and safety requirements before release. This progression reflects a broadly consistent pattern across recent Physical AI literature: general capability is built first, then progressively specialized, transferred, and hardened for real-world use.

Deployment in Physical AI requires multiple interconnected phases, including system integration, hardware deployment and calibration, safety verification, real-world commissioning, operational monitoring, and continuous maintenance and model updates, to ensure reliable, safe, and adaptive operation in dynamic environments Thakur et al. (2025). Policy evaluation assesses learned behavior against performance and safety criteria under real or near-real conditions, and automatic system identification Aljalbout et al. 4.2 Operationalization Government Elements into Each Life Cycle Components 

Operationalizing governance throughout the Physical AI lifecycle is a continuous, iterative process that requires cross-functional coordination internally and externally International Organization for Standardization (2025). For operationalizing traditional AI governance, there are international AI governance frameworks, including the OECD AI Principles, G20 AI Principles, the EU Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, and UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence. They provide practical guidance across the AI lifecycle at both the technical level, such as algorithm design, system architecture, documentation, and continuous monitoring, and the non-technical level, including governance structures, regulatory mechanisms, certification, education, and stakeholder engagement Vedashree et al. (2026). Many existing AI governance practices can be extended to Physical AI; however, they must be augmented with governance mechanisms that explicitly address embodiment, physical safety, human–robot interaction, environmental uncertainty, and continuous operational assurance throughout the system life cycle Riek & Howard (2014). 

4.2.1 Foundational Governance Factors 

Foundational governance for physical AI defines the core legal, operational, and ethical parameters required to manage autonomous systems that interact directly with the kinetic world. By codifying strict standards 16 for deterministic edge safety, human-centric values, localized sovereignty, and transparent audit trails, this framework establishes a unified mechanism to manage the lifecycle risks of embodied AI. 

Safety Assurance Physical AI transitions risk from digital outputs on a screen directly into human-shared kinetic environments, where algorithmic failures carry the immediate risk of physical harm or property damage PricewaterhouseCoopers (2026). For following standards, to operationalize safety as a governance principle, for example, the Risk–Rights–Rules (3R) framework guided operation with a Goal Structuring Notation (GSN)-based assurance case that links legal rights and regulatory requirements to explicit engineering evidence, including hazard analyzes, demographic fairness evaluations, drift monitoring, audit logs, human override records, procurement audit rights, and post-deployment monitoring plans. It further identifies lifecycle control points where sensing scope, inference transparency, actuation constraints, and human review must be enforced and continuously audited Niazi et al. (2026). For research, safety should be treated as a first-class objective throughout Physical AI research rather than being deferred until deployment Bak et al. (2021). Research prototypes interacting with the physical world should adopt deployment-grade safety practices, including formal verification, uncertainty quantification, runtime monitoring, scenario-based evaluation, standardized safety benchmarks, and human-in-the-loop validation Bak et al. (2021); Zhang et al. (2025a); Tang et al. (2026). Emerging frameworks further advocate lifecycle-oriented physical risk control by integrating safety assessment across pre-deployment design, runtime operation, and post-incident analysis, emphasizing continuous risk management throughout the system lifecycle Kojima et al. (2025). For design, AI architectures should explicitly reflect the structure of the physical world, rather than relying on a single end-to-end neural network. Scenario-aware hierarchical architectures, designing around physical scenarios rather than data distributions, embedding formal verification into system design, separating safety-critical decision making from low-level control, and prioritizing verifiability alongside model accuracy are promising design strategies for achieving safety-by-design in Physical AI systemsLiu et al. (2022). Safety-by-design can be achieved through a top-down engineering process that begins with system-level safety objectives rather than component-level performance metrics. Instead of optimizing perception or localization accuracy independently, system-level safety goals are translated into measurable requirements for individual AI components. These requirements are validated through simulation-based, scenario-driven testing that evaluates perception and planning modules under diverse hazardous conditions, ensuring that component performance contributes directly to end-to-end operational safety Singh et al. (2021). For data, benchmark datasets, such as SAFETextare develeped through human-in-the-loop approach and contains both safe and unsafe actions. It can be used to evaluate and train safety-aware language models Levy et al. (2022). Safety can be improved through continuous monitoring, fault diagnosis, and adaptive control. Intelligent sensing technologies combined with machine learning enable real-time state estimation, anomaly detection, and early fault identification, while data-driven fault diagnosis methods such as PCA, PLS, and observer-based techniques improve robustness under uncertainty and changing operating conditions. Safety is further enhanced through fault-tolerant control, plug-and-play control architectures, and reinforce-ment learning-based adaptive control, allowing systems to respond to failures, cyber attacks, and dynamic environments while maintaining safe operation Jiang et al. (2018). For model, system safety depends on cybersecurity Illiashenko et al. (2023). Model-centric approaches have increasingly been adopted to improve the safety of Physical AI systems by incorporating domain knowledge into the learning process. One important direction is to replace correlation-based learning with intervention-based reasoning using techniques such as Pearl’s do-operator and do-calculus. By explicitly modeling the effects of actions, this approach reduces unsafe decisions arising from spurious correlations and enables more reliable decision-making under distribution shifts and changing environments. Another promising direction is physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), which embed governing physical laws directly into the learning objective. By enforcing ordinary or partial differential equations during training, PINNs produce physically consistent predictions, improve robustness and generalization under limited data, and prevent physically implausible behaviors. Farahmand & Neu (2025). Unlike conventional anomaly detection methods, conformal prediction can be integrated with robot-environment simulations to construct runtime early warning systems that provide provable statistical safety guarantees. The resulting framework bounds the false negative rate, 17 ensuring that fewer than a predefined proportion ( ϵ) of unsafe situations occur without issuing an alert, thereby enabling reliable runtime safety monitoring for learning-enabled Physical AI systems Luo et al. (2024). In addition, in-Context Learning (ICL). In-context learning improves the physical safety of embodied AI by providing safety-related demonstrations that guide reasoning before action generation. On a drone-control benchmark, ICL increased GPT-3.5’s self-assurance from 12.5% to 87.2% and improved collision avoidance from 13.7% to 95.1%, demonstrating that safety-aware prompting can substantially reduce unsafe behaviors without retraining the underlying model Tang et al. (2026). For deployment, learning-enabled autonomous systems, safety is increasingly operationalized through runtime assurance rather than one-time validation, where runtime monitors continuously verify safety constraints and intervene by switching to verified fallback controllers or preventing unsafe actions when operational limits are exceeded. Studies in autonomous driving have demonstrated that such architectures can maintain safe operation despite unsafe outputs from learning-based controllers, while preserving system performance Chen et al. (2021). 

Human Value Alignment 

Physical AI systems operate in close proximity to humans, making human values such as autonomy, safety, trust, accessibility, and meaningful human control fundamental system requirements rather than optional design considerations Martinetti et al. (2021); International Organization for Standardization (2025). For research, human values should be incorporated throughout the Physical AI research lifecycle rather than being evaluated only after deployment. Recent embodied AI and robotics research advocates human-centered evaluation through real-world interaction studies, standardized safety benchmarks, and human-subject experiments that assess trust, usability, shared autonomy, and human oversight alongside task performance Tang et al. (2026); Kojima et al. (2025). Tools and frames such as Human–Robot Interaction (HRI) to integrate human well-being, social norms, ethical principles, and interaction-driven values directly into the lifecycle. A three-phase, closed-loop methodology for embedding human values begins with a scoping review that systematically examines existing HRI literature to identify how human values have been discussed. These preliminary findings are then refined through focus groups with philosophy and ethics experts, resulting in a validated taxonomy of HRI values and ethical considerations. Finally, these insights are translated into practice through the HRI Value Compass, a web-based design tool that helps HRI researchers identify relevant values, ethical concerns, and design opportunities during robot development. For design, co-design with end users, shared autonomy, meaningful human control, transparent human–robot interaction, and accessible interfaces enable robots to collaborate safely and effectively with diverse users while respecting human intentions, capabilities, and decision authority Martinetti et al. (2021); International Organization for Standardization (2025). In addition, social value should be incorporated at the design stage by engaging stakeholders early and translating societal values into engineering requirements through methodologies such as Value Sensitive Design (VSD), Human-Centered Design (HCD), and participatory design. VSD provides a systematic framework comprising conceptual, empirical, and technical investigations that identify stakeholder values, evaluate value trade-offs, and embed these values into system architecture, algorithms, and interfaces Khosravy et al. (2024). Depending on the application, human–robot collaboration can be organized with different levels of robot autonomy and human involvement, ranging from elementary HRC, where robots assist routine tasks, to robot-in-lead and human-in-lead modes that balance automation and human expertise, ultimately progressing toward advanced HRC, where collaborative robots and AI augment human decision-making through continuous learning, adaptability, efficiency, and safety Ma & Saenz (2025). For data, human-centered datasets should include diverse users, environments, interaction scenarios, and physical abilities to improve accessibility and reduce bias. Human-in-the-loop data collection and participatory annotation allow robot behaviors to better reflect real user needs and preferences, particularly for people with disabilities and other underrepresented user groups Tang et al. (2026). Learning from demonstration data captures human motions as skeletal trajectories and uses them to train reinforcement learning models, enabling 18 robots to efficiently learn complex tasks from human demonstrations rather than explicit programming Doncieux et al. (2022). For model development, techniques such as fairness-aware learning, Explainable AI (XAI), and human-in-the-loop (HITL) approaches improve transparency, trust, and appropriate human oversight. During deployment and operation, governance mechanisms including audit trails, algorithmic impact assessments, and clearly defined accountability structures ensure responsible system management. Finally, during post-deployment monitoring, continuous social impact assessment, user feedback loops, participatory monitoring, and longitudinal evaluation enable Physical AI systems to measure societal outcomes and continuously improve their positive impact. For deployment, human values should be embedded into robot decision making through value alignment, uncertainty-aware reasoning, and human preference modeling. Embodied AI systems increasingly incorporate human demonstrations, safety-aware prompting, and interactive feedback to better interpret human intentions, remain responsive to human intervention, and reduce unsafe behaviors during task execution Tang et al. (2026); Kojima et al. (2025). For deployment, human values are operationalized through continuous human oversight, runtime human override, transparent robot behavior, and post-deployment user feedback. Recent physical risk control frameworks advocate continuous monitoring across pre-deployment, runtime, and post-incident phases to ensure that Physical AI systems remain aligned with evolving human expectations throughout their operational lifecycle Kojima et al. (2025); International Organization for Standardization (2025). 

Transparency and Accountability Unlike purely virtual software, physical AI requires continuous, ambient collection of localized sensor telemetry, spatial maps, and environmental data, necessitating rigid data sovereignty frameworks to prevent unauthorized cross-border exfiltration of critical infrastructure data Everest Group (2026). Real-time operational transparency demands comprehensive logging of the entire "perception-to-actuation" loop, making localized navigation decisions and sensor logicfully auditable after an incident PricewaterhouseCoopers (2026); IEEE Operational Standard P4501 (2026). Furthermore, accountability must be hardcoded into the organizational structure, resolving the complex legal liabilities that emerge when physical failures occur by mapping an unbroken chain of responsibility across hardware developers, model designers, and local site operators World Economic Forum (2026); PricewaterhouseCoopers (2026). 

4.2.2 Research Governance Factors 

Research governance establishes the scientific foundation upon which trustworthy Physical AI systems are built. Because embodied AI systems operate in safety-critical and highly dynamic environments, unreliable or irreproducible research can propagate into downstream engineering decisions and eventually affect real-world safety. 

Reliability concerns whether experimental findings consistently produce the claimed behavior under different operating conditions rather than only under carefully controlled laboratory settings. Recent sim-to-real robot benchmarking work makes this concern concrete: evaluations of generalist manipulation and perception policies show substantial, often underreported, performance degradation once testing moves beyond the training distribution into varied textures, object placements, lighting, and sensor-noise conditions Jangir et al. (2025), and dedicated corruption-robustness benchmarks for autonomous driving perception show that reported accuracy on clean benchmark data does not reliably predict performance under real sensor anomalies, weather, or lighting variation Wang et al. (2025b). Much of the recent methodological effort in Physical AI research has gone directly into narrowing this reliability gap rather than merely documenting it: photorealistic real-to-sim reconstruction pipelines aim to make simulated training and evaluation data reliably predictive of real-world manipulation performance Han et al. (2025), real-to-sim-to-real loops that derive reward signals from vision-language models have been proposed specifically to keep policies grounded in real-world outcomes rather than simulator artifacts Patel et al. (2025), and controlled studies isolating a single physical variable — static friction — have shown that this one property alone can materially change whether a reinforcement-learned manipulation policy transfers reliably from simulation to hardware Hu et al. (2025). Therefore, operationalizing reliability requires researchers to evaluate models under varying lighting conditions, weather, terrain, sensor noise, and hardware platforms while reporting uncertainty estimates, confidence 19 intervals, and failure cases in addition to average performance metrics. For example, rather than reporting object detection accuracy solely on a benchmark dataset, researchers developing an autonomous warehouse robot should demonstrate that the perception system maintains reliable performance when warehouse layouts change, shelves are partially occluded, lighting conditions vary, or sensors experience temporary degradation. Such evaluations provide stronger evidence that the underlying scientific contribution is applicable to realistic deployment environments. 

Reproducibility is closely related to reliability, which ensures that independent researchers can replicate published findings using the same methodology. The robotics community has documented its own version of the reproducibility crisis: closed-source, simplified, or specially tailored hardware and software in laboratory demonstrators renders reproduction of most published robotics experiments difficult, a concern serious enough that a unified conceptual framework (MIRRER) has been proposed specifically to formalize and jointly evaluate reproducibility, replicability, and generalizability in robotics research Norton & Flynn (2024). The problem is not confined to any one subfield — reviews of surgical robotics have likewise found that reproducibility is not yet mainstream practice, with patenting restrictions, safety constraints, and closed hardware compounding the difficulty of replication Faragasso & Bonsignorio (2023), while IEEE Robotics & Automation Magazine’s Reproducible Articles initiative was created specifically to encourage detailed, replicable experimental protocols in intelligent robotics Bonsignorio (2017). For Physical AI, reproducibility extends beyond releasing source code to documenting simulation environments, robot hardware specifications, sensor configurations, software versions, training pipelines, and evaluation protocols. Researchers can operationalize reproducibility by publishing datasets, simulation assets, preprocessing pipelines, hyperparameters, random seeds, and benchmark scripts alongside their publications. For instance, a manipulation policy trained using NVIDIA Isaac Sim should include detailed simulator configurations, robot kinematics, object models, and task definitions so that independent research groups can reproduce the experimental results before extending the proposed method. Such practices improve scientific credibility while accelerating cumulative progress across the Physical AI research community. 

4.2.3 Design Governance Factors 

Governance should be embedded during the system design phase before implementation decisions constrain future development. Early design decisions determine system objectives, operational boundaries, acceptable risks, and stakeholder priorities, thereby influencing downstream data collection, model development, and deployment strategies. 

inclusiveness seeks to ensure that Physical AI systems reflect the needs, expectations, and values of the diverse individuals and communities that interact with them rather than optimizing exclusively for technical performance. Value-sensitive approaches applied directly to embodied systems illustrate how this can be done in practice: participatory design studies for socially assistive robots have used structured value-elicitation methods with real stakeholder groups to surface conflicting priorities before a system is built, rather than after deployment Poprcova et al. (2025), and multi-stakeholder empirical studies of socially assistive robots in elder care have shown that patients, family caregivers, and clinical staff often prioritize different values (autonomy versus safety, companionship versus efficiency), values that only surface through direct, structured elicitation Zhong & Schmiedel (2025). Operationally, inclusiveness can be incorporated through stakeholder mapping, participatory design workshops, interviews, usability testing, and iterative human-in-the-loop design reviews involving both technical and non-technical users. For example, when designing a hospital delivery robot, developers should engage nurses, physicians, patients, maintenance personnel, and hospital administrators during requirement elicitation to identify workflow constraints, accessibility concerns, communication preferences, and safety expectations that may not emerge through purely engineering-driven design. 

Generalizability complements inclusiveness by ensuring that system designs remain applicable across diverse deployment contexts instead of being optimized for a single environment. Physical AI systems frequently encounter distribution shifts after deployment due to changing environments, hardware upgrades, or evolving operational requirements. The MIRRER framework treats generalizability as a formally measurable robotics capability — the ability of a robot to perform a task under varied contexts — precisely because the field has historically lacked shared definitions or protocols for evaluating it Norton & Flynn (2024), and empirical 20 real-to-sim evaluations of vision-language-action policies confirm the concern in practice: performance declines markedly once policies are evaluated in environments or object configurations beyond their training distribution, indicating that today’s "generalist" policies still specialize to their training data Sedlacek et al. (2025). Domain-randomization and domain-adaptation techniques have been proposed specifically to close this gap by exposing manipulation policies to deliberately varied simulated conditions during training Jin et al. (2026). Two concrete strands of Physical AI research illustrate what governance-relevant generalizability looks like in practice: cross-embodiment work such as Open X-Embodiment has shown that pooling manipulation data across many distinct robot bodies and training a single policy on the combined corpus improves generalization beyond what any single-embodiment dataset can provide Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2024), and follow-on architectures have pursued the same goal by explicitly sharing representations or attention mechanisms across heterogeneous robot morphologies rather than training a separate model per platform Doshi et al. (2024); Wang et al. (2024). A second, complementary strand targets generalization across open-ended language and object categories rather than across hardware: composable value-map and semantic-field approaches allow a manipulation policy to act on objects and instructions never seen during training by grounding pretrained vision-language representations directly in the robot’s 3D workspace Huang et al. (2023b); Shafiullah et al. (2023). Consequently, governance should encourage modular architectures and design validation across multiple representative scenarios rather than optimizing solely for a single benchmark environment. In practice, generalizability can be operationalized through cross-domain validation, modular software architectures, simulation-to-real evaluation, and testing under diverse environmental conditions before deployment. For example, an agricultural harvesting robot designed initially for apple orchards should be evaluated for its ability to adapt to vineyards or citrus farms without requiring substantial redesign of its perception and manipulation systems. Such evaluations provide confidence that the design remains robust under changing operational contexts and future deployment scenarios. 

4.2.4 Data Governance Factors 

Data governance is particularly important for Physical AI because embodied systems continuously collect multimodal information from their surrounding environments through cameras, LiDAR, microphones, inertial sensors, and positioning systems. Unlike traditional AI applications that operate on static datasets, Physical AI systems often collect data throughout their operational lifetime, creating additional privacy and accountability challenges. Robotics-specific studies of household and social robots show these challenges are distinctive to embodiment: because social robots rely on rich multimodal sensing — audio, video, motion, proximity, and affective cues — they can capture information beyond what a user explicitly intends to share, and their personalized behavior can itself leak sensitive information to bystanders who never interacted with the robot directly Andriella et al. (2026). Empirical work benchmarking privacy-relevant decision-making in household robots found that users’ own privacy expectations for what a robot may record, process, or share frequently diverge from what current AI decision-making systems would actually do, underscoring the need for explicit, engineered privacy safeguards rather than reliance on model judgment alone Sullivan et al. (2025). Robot-specific design work has responded with concrete privacy-by-design techniques — on-device processing, real-time user feedback and intervention interfaces, and privacy-sensitive sensor design — built specifically for domestic and social robot deployments Sullivan & Mutlu (2025). Operationalizing these principles includes implementing privacy-by-design strategies such as edge processing, data minimization, informed consent mechanisms, encryption, anonymization, and comprehensive documentation describing data usage. For example, an indoor service robot operating in an assisted-living facility may perform facial recognition locally on the device instead of transmitting raw video streams to cloud servers while simultaneously informing residents about the categories of data collected and their intended purposes. 

Traceability complements transparency by ensuring that every dataset, preprocessing step, annotation procedure, and model version can be traced throughout the AI lifecycle. This is a distinctly harder problem in Physical AI than in conventional ML pipelines: robot policy iteration generates a messy, embodied data ecosystem in which a rollout is more than a video, a dataset is more than a folder of accepted episodes, and a trained policy is more than a checkpoint — each is an artifact that must be linked across robot configuration, task context, sensor observations, human review, dataset admission, training provenance, and deployment decisions, a governance problem serious enough to motivate purpose-built agent-native lineage-tracking systems for robot learning Luo et al. (2026). IEEE’s transparency standard for autonomous 21 and intelligent systems similarly calls for autonomous systems to make their reasoning and data provenance auditable to operators and, where appropriate, to affected users IEEE (2021). In practice, traceability can be operationalized using dataset version control, metadata repositories, automated lineage tracking, and comprehensive audit logs that document how data evolve over time. For instance, following an unexpected navigation failure in an autonomous mobile robot, engineers should be able to identify the specific sensor recordings, annotation versions, preprocessing pipeline, and trained model responsible for the observed behavior. Such end-to-end provenance substantially improves post-incident investigation while facilitating continuous system improvement. 

4.2.5 Model Governance Factors 

Model governance focuses on ensuring that learned decision-making processes remain understandable, equitable, and aligned with human expectations. 

Explainability is particularly important for Physical AI because model predictions directly influence physical actions that may affect human safety. Work specifically on eplainable Autonomous Robots (XAR) has produced concrete demonstrations of this: a DARPA-funded study had a robot narrate the steps it took while performing a manipulation task (opening a medicine bottle), and found that even simple "what"-level explanations measurably increased human participants’ trust in the robot’s behavior, though the researchers cautioned that trust must be appropriately calibrated rather than simply maximized Ackerman (2019); Sanneman (2020). More recent work integrating large language models into robot explanation pipelines has focused on grounding generated explanations in the robot’s actual internal state and sensor logs, rather than generic post-hoc narratives, specifically to preserve the link between what a robot says and what it did Fernández-Becerra et al. (2026). A closely related line of work treats explainability as inseparable from failure detection: vision-language-model monitors have been developed specifically to watch an executing robot policy, flag the point at which a task-relevant constraint has been violated, and produce a human-readable account of what went wrong and why, rather than a bare failure code Zhou et al. (2025). Operationally, explainability can be incorporated through interpretable policy representations, attention visualization, feature attribution methods, causal reasoning, natural-language explanations, and comprehensive decision logging. For example, if a collaborative robot unexpectedly interrupts an assembly task, the system should indicate whether the interruption resulted from obstacle detection, excessive force sensing, or predicted collision risk rather than reporting only a generic system error. Such explanations improve debugging efficiency while increasing user trust and facilitating regulatory oversight. 

Bias and fairness remain equally important because Physical AI systems interact directly with diverse human populations and physical environments. Biased training data, unrepresentative simulation environments, or demographic imbalances may produce unequal system performance across user groups, potentially resulting in unsafe or discriminatory behavior. A Science Robotics commentary has documented this directly in embodied systems: object-detection systems used in autonomous vehicles have shown uniformly poorer performance detecting pedestrians with darker skin tones, and commercial facial-recognition APIs deployed in robotic platforms have exhibited measurable racial bias Howard & Kennedy III (2021). Robotics-specific audits of perceptual components used in human-robot interaction have extended this finding: facial-landmark and expression-recognition models used for engagement and affect estimation show degraded accuracy for older adults and other underrepresented groups, a disparity that can propagate directly into inequitable or inappropriate robot responses Parte et al. (2026), and broader surveys of bias in robot learning show that even when protected attributes such as age or gender are never used as explicit model inputs, robots can still learn to encode and act on them implicitly through raw sensor and demonstration data Vöneky et al. (2024). In practice, organizations should curate representative datasets, evaluate subgroup performance, perform robustness testing across diverse environments, and periodically reassess model behavior after deployment. For example, a domestic assistance robot should demonstrate comparable recognition and navigation performance for elderly individuals, wheelchair users, children, and individuals with different body sizes rather than optimizing exclusively for healthy adults. Continuous fairness monitoring is particularly important because deployment environments often evolve over time, introducing new demographic distributions that were absent during initial model development. Model governance in this sense also extends beyond demographic fairness to adversarial robustness: recent work has shown that LLM-controlled robots can be deliberately "jailbroken" 22 into executing unsafe or harmful physical actions that their language backbone would normally refuse to describe, motivating dedicated safety-guardrail layers that sit between a language model’s reasoning and the robot’s actuators Robey et al. (2025), alongside broader efforts to encode an explicit, checkable set of behavioral constraints, a kind of "robot constitution", directly into general-purpose embodied foundation models Gemini Robotics Team (2025). 

4.2.6 Deployment Governance Factors 

Governance responsibilities continue after deployment because Physical AI systems operate in dynamic environments where software, hardware, and environmental conditions continually change. 

Resilience therefore refers to the ability of a Physical AI system to anticipate, withstand, recover from, and adapt to unexpected disruptions while maintaining safe operational behavior. A systems-engineering mapping of assurance techniques for learning-enabled autonomous systems identifies resilience engineering as a distinct discipline that accommodates sensor inaccuracies arising from measurement limitations, dust, debris, or adversarial conditions, allowing a system to recognize degraded performance and adapt — for example, by shifting from perception-based to odometry-based navigation Ellis et al. (2023). Empirical work on robot swarms reinforces the need for this to be predictive rather than purely reactive: faults such as dust-related sensor degradation tend to accumulate gradually rather than occur as sudden step changes, so anomaly-detection methods that catch early-stage degradation before it becomes a critical failure meaningfully improve long-term autonomy O’Keeffe & Millard (2023); Khalastchi et al. (2015). Resilience failures are not only mechanical: language-model-driven planners can also silently drift out of alignment with what the robot is actually able to execute, which has motivated methods that explicitly detect this plan-execution misalignment and ground the language model’s next decision back in the robot’s real state Guo et al. (2023), as well as corrective-planning frameworks that use the detected mismatch to revise the plan online rather than aborting the task outright Joublin et al. (2024). Rather than relying solely on pre-deployment verification, resilient governance requires continuous monitoring, anomaly detection, predictive maintenance, digital twins, fallback controllers, and periodic safety validation throughout the operational lifecycle. For example, if an autonomous warehouse robot experiences degraded LiDAR performance due to dust accumulation, a resilient system should automatically transition to alternative localization methods, reduce operating speed, notify human operators, and continue operating safely until maintenance can be performed. Such adaptive responses reduce operational risk while supporting long-term system reliability. 

Sustainability extends governance beyond immediate technical performance by considering the environmental, economic, and societal impacts of Physical AI systems throughout their lifecycle. A recent manifesto formally proposes Sustainability Robotics as its own research discipline, arguing that sustainable robot design must account for environmental footprint across the full physical lifecycle — material selection, energy consumption, and manufacturing — rather than software efficiency alone, since robots are physical products with a materials and end-of-life burden that purely digital AI systems do not have Song et al. (2026). This lifecycle view has already been applied concretely: a case study integrating full life-cycle assessment into the design of an urban municipal-waste service robot showed how environmental, social, and economic sustainability criteria can be built into requirements from the earliest design stage rather than retrofitted afterward van der Schoor & Göhlich (2023), while a roadmap for climate-relevant robotics research cautions that the emissions from building, training, and operating growing fleets of physical robots are a real sustainability cost that must be weighed against the climate benefits robots can provide, even though this cost is still difficult to generalize across applications and platforms Papalia et al. (2025). At the model level, a growing body of Physical AI research targets the energy cost of inference directly, since a vision-language-action policy that must run continuously on onboard hardware has a very different efficiency profile than a model queried occasionally in the cloud: dynamic-inference methods skip unnecessary computation on easy control steps to reduce the energy and latency cost of running large multimodal policies on a physical robot Yue et al. (2024), action-aware pruning techniques cut redundant computation from vision-language-action inference without materially degrading task success Wang et al. (2025a), and reinforcement-learning-based recovery methods restore the performance lost when a policy is aggressively compressed for cheaper onboard deployment Chen et al. (2025c). Operationally, sustainability can be incorporated by monitoring energy consumption, optimizing inference efficiency, employing model compression techniques, implementing predictive maintenance strategies, 23 and conducting lifecycle assessments that consider manufacturing, operation, maintenance, and end-of-life disposal. For example, an autonomous delivery robot fleet can continuously optimize routing strategies to reduce battery consumption while predictive maintenance extends hardware lifespan and minimizes electronic waste. Integrating sustainability metrics into routine operational monitoring enables organizations to evaluate not only whether Physical AI systems remain technically effective but also whether they continue to create long-term environmental and societal value. 

# 5 Future Considerations 

The trajectory of Physical AI governance is moving away from fragmented, voluntary guidance and toward layered frameworks that combine technical standards, sector-specific regulation, and adaptive oversight mechanisms. 

Layered Framework with Metrics A layered governance framework enables different stakehold-ers—including researchers, developers, operators, regulators, and end users—to understand their respective responsibilities and governance requirements within specific application scenarios. To support continuous improvement and accountability, governance should also incorporate measurable metrics and quantitative evaluation methods that assess safety, reliability, robustness, and human-centered outcomes throughout the life cycle Winfield & Jirotka (2021). 

Enforcement Physical AI governance should extend beyond voluntary ethical guidelines by incorporating community consensus, technical standards, and legal regulations to ensure consistent implementation and enforceable accountability. Responsible Physical AI requires close coordination between research, governance, and standardization to ensure that technological advances are matched by appropriate safeguards. Effective governance depends on collaboration among the AI research community, policymakers, and standards organizations, where researchers identify emerging risks, governments establish regulatory frameworks, and standards bodies develop technical specifications to support the safe and trustworthy deployment of Physical AI systems Mezgár & Váncza (2022). 

Adaptive Governance Physical AI systems continuously learn, interact with changing environments, and may receive model or software updates after deployment. Governance should therefore evolve from static compliance toward adaptive governance that supports continuous risk assessment, monitoring, and iterative updates throughout the system life cycle rather than one-time certification National Institute of Standards and Technology (2023). 

# 6 Conclusion 

As Physical AI moves artificial intelligence off the screen and into embodied systems that share physical space with people, its failures acquire immediate, real-world consequences that existing digital-AI governance frameworks were not designed to contain. This survey has argued that governing Physical AI requires treating governance not as a compliance step appended after deployment, but as a concern that spans the entire life cycle of an embodied system. To this end, we made two contributions. First, we synthesized a Physical AI governance framework (P-Gov) that organizes the field around five fundamental principles—Robust & Safe Operation, Human-Centered Values, Integrity/Privacy/Equity, Accountability & Oversight, and Sustainability—together with their constituent sub-components, clarifying how each principle differs from its conventional-AI counterpart once embodiment, physical safety, and continuous human interaction are taken into account. Second, we distilled an end-to-end Physical AI life cycle (E-PAL) comprising Research, Design, Data, Model, and Deployment, and surveyed how the governance principles are operationalized at each stage, translating high-level values into concrete engineering practices such as sim-to-real reliability testing, data provenance and traceability, explainable and bias-audited policies, runtime resilience monitoring, and life-cycle sustainability assessment. Bringing these together, we organized the operationalized governance elements into a three-tier life-cycle framework: a Fundamental tier of cross-cutting principles, a Knowledge Generation tier covering Research and Design, and a Building, Deployment, and Operation tier covering Data, Model, and Deployment. This 24 framework is descriptive rather than causal—it does not claim that satisfying each element guarantees a well-governed system, only that omitting the stage-specific form of an element leaves a corresponding governance gap. We therefore see several open directions: validating the framework against real development processes, developing stage-specific and quantitative metrics for each governance element, and moving from static, one-time certification toward adaptive governance that co-evolves with systems that continuously learn and are updated after deployment. We hope this survey offers a shared vocabulary and a systematic foundation that helps researchers, developers, operators, and regulators advance Physical AI in a way that is both scientifically productive and socially responsible. 25 References
